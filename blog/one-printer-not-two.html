One Printer, Not Two: A Lesson in System Design from UPS

A receipt printed on shipping-label stock, and what it says about building one system instead of two.

AAIM Focus
AAIMK

I dropped off a prepaid package at the UPS Store the other day. Simple errand. Before I left, I asked for a receipt — not because I needed one, but because if the shipment ever goes sideways, I want a paper trail I can point to. Nine times out of ten I never look at it again. But that tenth time matters.

What they handed me wasn't a receipt in the “corner store, buy a Snickers bar” sense. It was printed on the same heavy, glossy stock as a shipping label — the kind of material that's built to survive a conveyor belt and stick to a box for a week. My first reaction was almost indignant on the company's behalf: why would you waste expensive label stock on a receipt nobody asked to see?

Then I actually thought about it, and the logic flipped completely.

Label stock is expensive because it has to be. Sticking a durable, scannable label onto a package is core to UPS's business — that's not a place to cut corners. But maintaining a second printing system, with its own cheap paper stock, its own printer, its own supply chain, its own thing to run out of and fix when it jams — that's not core to the business at all. It's overhead in service of a transaction that happens maybe once a week per customer.

So the real decision wasn't “cheap paper vs. expensive paper.” It was “one system vs. two systems.” And once you frame it that way, the expensive-looking receipt is actually the cheaper choice. Running one printer that does everything, even if the per-unit cost is a little higher, beats running two printers where one of them exists purely to save pennies on stock you already have plenty of infrastructure for.

The same decision shows up constantly in data pipelines

This is a pattern I run into all the time in ETL and analytics work. There's always a tempting argument for building a second, “lighter” system for some subset of the work — a separate lightweight extract for the small tables, a bespoke script for the “simple” reports, a parallel process because the main pipeline “feels like overkill” for this one use case.

And sometimes that instinct is right. But a lot of the time, it's the receipt-paper trap: you're optimizing a transactional cost while ignoring the much bigger cost of maintaining an entirely separate system — one with its own failure modes, its own documentation, its own person who has to remember how it works six months from now when it breaks.

The question worth asking isn't “what's the cheapest way to handle this specific case?” It's “can our existing system absorb this, even if it's not the perfectly optimized path for it?” Usually, the answer is yes — and the few extra cents (or compute cycles, or minutes of runtime) you spend by not building a second bespoke pipeline are cheap compared to the ongoing tax of maintaining two systems instead of one.

Simplicity isn't always the option that looks cheapest line-item by line-item. Sometimes it's the option that costs a little more per transaction and a lot less in brainpower, forever.

Mapping to the AAIM Method

Automate

This is the Automate step showing its teeth. Automation only pays off if it's something you build once and trust — the moment you're running two pipelines instead of one, you've doubled the thing you have to trust, document, and fix. A single system that absorbs the occasional awkward case is worth more than two systems that are each individually a little more efficient.
